Tax and penalties: what is actually owed, and what is actually fined

Two systems, four questions. China: is the conversion taxed, and is the source taxed? US: is the receipt income, and is it reported? The answers are short. The penalty columns are what make the paperwork worth doing.

Short answer. Converting CNY to USD is not a taxable event in either country. The money's source may already have been taxed in China (salary, property gain, dividend) and is taxable in the US whether or not you move it. The fines attach to how you move it and whether you report the account - not to the conversion.

The two worked comparisons that matter

Converting USD 400,000 of already-taxed principal

PRC income tax on the conversion: none identified - no category in S10 Art. 2. US income tax on the conversion: none on principal; the section 988 currency leg is a personal transaction, so no gain is recognised unless it would exceed USD 200 (S15 (e)). Known transfer cost: $915.77 (0.2289%). The only large US number in the file is the reporting penalty if the account was never disclosed: USD 16,536 non-willful per report or USD 165,353 willful (S19).

Selling a mainland property with a gain

The PRC rate is 20% of (proceeds - original value - reasonable expenses) (S10 Arts. 3(3), 6(5)). No gain is supplied in this project, so no amount is shown: the rate is published, the base is personal (F12). In the US the same disposal is a taxable event with the gain computed in dollars; a foreign tax credit may reduce double taxation if the PRC tax qualifies.

Reporting calendar for a US citizen holding the account

  1. During the year: keep statements showing the maximum balance, because both FBAR and Form 8938 ask for maximum values (S16, S17).
  2. 15 April: FBAR due; Form 8938 with the return; Form 3520 if a reportable foreign gift was received (S18).
  3. 15 October: automatic FBAR extension, no request needed (S16).
  4. Five years: keep FBAR records from the due date (S16).
