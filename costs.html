Breaking down the "conversion penalty"

The premise was that converting triggers a large tax. It doesn't. What exists is six different costs wearing one name — and the biggest of them has nothing to do with converting at all.

Why there is no total Two of the largest real-world costs — the bank's FX bid-ask spread and correspondent-bank fees — are set commercially, published by no official source, and vary by bank, ticket size, channel and customer tier. Quoting a percentage for them would be fabrication. So those rows are deliberately blank and marked unverified. An article that hands you a single confident "conversion penalty %" is guessing. Which costs apply to which route

Reading the asymmetry

The legitimate route

Chinese tax on conversion: zero. Chinese IIT on the underlying gain: real but proportionate (20% of the gain, not the proceeds). Spread and fees: unknown but small in relative terms. Cost scales roughly linearly with the amount.

The illegitimate route

SAFE fines bite on the whole amount, not the gain: up to 30%, and 30–100% if serious, plus confiscation and possible criminal referral. Cost can equal or exceed the entire balance.

The comparison that matters On a $100,000 balance, a 1% spread costs about $1,000. The same balance unreported for three years is three FBAR violations — up to $49,608 if non-willful, and far more if willful. Reporting compliance is worth roughly fifty times more than shopping for a better FX rate. Do that first.
On the $200 figure IRC §988(e)(2) sets a USD 200 de minimis for foreign-currency gain on a personal transaction, and §988(e)(1) removes personal transactions from §988 altogether. The practical effect is that converting your own savings is not meant to create US income — but a personal FX loss is likewise not deductible. The interaction of (e)(1) and (e)(2) is genuinely technical; get a CPA to confirm the characterisation for your facts rather than relying on this summary.
Not advice. Penalty amounts quoted are statutory or inflation-adjusted maximums; actual assertions depend on facts and circumstances and on the IRS's or SAFE's discretion. Reasonable-cause defences exist for several of them. Nothing here is a prediction of what you would owe.
