Converting CNY to USD from a mainland Chinese bank account

A research dossier for a US citizen with renminbi held in China: every channel, its legal basis, its cost, and its penalty exposure, each figure cited to an official source.

China levies no tax on the act of converting CNY into USD. Nothing in the Individual Income Tax Law, the Enterprise Income Tax Law, the Regulation on Foreign Exchange Administration, the Measures for the Administration of Individual Foreign Exchange or the Detailed Rules imposes a tax, levy or conversion charge on exchanging renminbi for foreign currency. The obstacle the user describes as a 'considerable amount of taxes' is not a conversion tax. It is one of three different things, and separating them is the whole analysis.

2 routes

The three questions that decide everything

  • Is the money current-account income or capital? Art.13 of the Measures opens an uncapped route for legitimate current-account income earned in China by an overseas individual. Capital-account receipts need SAFE approval or registration. S01 S02
  • Has the Chinese tax on the underlying event been paid and evidenced? A tax voucher is a statutory precondition to the conversion, and Art.15 of the Individual Income Tax Law blocks registration of a property or equity transfer until the tax-paid certificate is produced. S02 S13
  • Is the route lawful? The administrative fine scales are up to 30% and 30% to 100% for serious cases, and the criminal threshold for illegal foreign exchange dealing is an illegal business amount of RMB 5,000,000. S03 S12

How to read this site

Every numeric cell in every table carries a link to the entry in the source register it was read from, with the article number. Where a value could not be confirmed against an official source the cell is left blank and carries an unverified badge linking to the Flagged for Review page. No value anywhere on this site has been inferred, estimated or carried over from a secondary source. The methodology page states the full standard and its limitations.

This is research, published with its sources, not advice. It is not legal, tax or investment advice and creates no professional relationship. Chinese foreign exchange practice varies by branch and by officer, several official instruments conflict with each other as recorded on the Flagged for Review page, and both the Chinese and the US positions depend on facts about the origin of the funds that are not known here. The correct next step for a material balance is a written opinion from a PRC-qualified lawyer and a US-qualified international tax adviser, taken together, on the specific facts.
