The conversion penalty, broken down

Six cost layers, the statutory penalty scales, and 23 cases SAFE published itself, with fine rates computed only where both official figures share a currency.

China levies no tax on the act of converting CNY into USD. Nothing in the Individual Income Tax Law, the Enterprise Income Tax Law, the Regulation on Foreign Exchange Administration, the Measures for the Administration of Individual Foreign Exchange or the Detailed Rules imposes a tax, levy or conversion charge on exchanging renminbi for foreign currency. The obstacle the user describes as a 'considerable amount of taxes' is not a conversion tax. It is one of three different things, and separating them is the whole analysis.

The cost of getting renminbi to US dollars decomposes into six layers. Layers 1 to 4 are the cost of doing it lawfully. Layers 5 and 6 are the cost of not doing so, and they are quantified from SAFE's own published enforcement cases rather than estimated. Percentages in the case table are computed as fine divided by amount moved and are shown ONLY where both official figures are expressed in the same currency. Where the amount moved is in USD, CAD or HKD and the fine is in RMB, the percentage is left null because computing it would require an exchange rate that is not fixed by any source in this register. No rate has been assumed.

What the case data shows

Finding

Where both figures are in RMB, published fines for illegal FX dealing through underground banks cluster tightly at 7%, 8% and 12% of the amount moved.

Evidence

7.00%, 8.00%, 8.00%, 12.01%, 12.02%, 12.03% across six published individual cases.

Why it matters

These sit well inside the Art.45 statutory band of up to 30%, and far below the 30% to 100% band reserved for serious circumstances. The observed administrative cost of an underground-bank route, before criminal exposure and before confiscation of illegal gains, is therefore roughly 7% to 12% of the amount moved.

Sources: S10 S11 S03

Finding

Splitting other people's annual quotas is charged as evasion of FX supervision under Art.39, not merely as a quota breach.

Evidence

Every published splitting case cites Measures Art.7 as the rule breached and Regulation Art.39 as the penalty basis, with an order to repatriate the funds within a time limit.

Why it matters

The repatriation order is the commercially decisive part: the money does not stay offshore. A fine is payable and the funds are ordered back.

Sources: S09 S10 S11 S03

Finding

Facilitating banks are penalised separately.

Evidence

A bank was fined and had RMB 1,000,000 confiscated for processing split purchases across 303 individuals' quotas; another RMB 800,000 for processing payments against false bills of lading.

Why it matters

Banks have their own exposure, which explains the conservative and inconsistent authenticity reviews individual customers experience at the counter.

Sources: S10 S03

Finding

The criminal threshold for illegal FX dealing is low relative to the sums in question.

Evidence

Fashi [2019] No.1 sets 'serious circumstances' at an illegal business amount of RMB 5,000,000 or illegal gains of RMB 100,000, and 'especially serious' at RMB 25,000,000 or RMB 500,000.

Why it matters

A single underground-bank transaction moving a mid-six-figure USD sum crosses the criminal threshold. The administrative fines above are the better outcome, not the expected one.
