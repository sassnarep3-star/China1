Every route, with pros and cons

21 routes across 7 categories — 17 lawful or conditional, 4 prohibited and documented only so they can be refused. A route is not a permission: a bank is a rail, and the entitlement to move the money is a separate legal question.

The distinction that decides your case: whether the money is current-account money (salary, services, investment income you can document) or capital-account money (savings accumulated, property proceeds, invested capital). The first is routine; the second needs an approval route. Which one you have was never supplied, so both are laid out.

Route detail

Bank of China wire: buy USD, send to your US account

Lawful

中国银行 购汇+电汇 · cost basis: Priced end-to-end from BOC's own published board and tariff plus a Chase receiving charge. Intermediary bank fees are excluded because no first-party figure was found.

Walk into (or log into) BOC, convert CNY to USD inside your FX account, then telegraphic-transfer it to your own US account. Up to USD 50,000/day outward from an FX savings account is ID-only; above that the bank must see genuine-transaction documents.

Pros
  • Fully documented, ordinary banking; no approval queue for the ID-only tier.
  • Fees are published and capped (CNY 260 commission + CNY 150 cable at the top end).
  • Receives a bank-to-bank audit trail that supports later US reporting.
  • Largest branch network in China; works in English at major branches.
Cons
  • The USD 50,000 annual total is per person per year and applies to settlement and purchase separately.
  • Above the ID-only tier the bank decides whether your documents are genuine; a refusal is not appealable in the moment.
  • Intermediary and receiving bank charges are deducted from the principal and are not published by BOC.
  • The counter rate is set by the bank, not by the PBOC mid-rate.
  • China tax: No tax on the act of converting. Tax attaches to the underlying income or gain: 20% flat on asset-transfer income and interest/dividends, 3%-45% progressive on salary (IIT Law Art. 3).
  • US tax: No tax on moving your own after-tax principal. FX gain on a personal transaction is not recognised unless it exceeds USD 200 (26 USC 988(e)(2)). Interest and dividends earned in China remain taxable worldwide.
  • US reporting: FBAR (FinCEN 114) if aggregate > USD 10,000 at any time; Form 8938 if thresholds met; Form 1116 for foreign tax credit; FinCEN 105 if you instead carry cash > USD 10,000

HSBC China wire (Premier / Advance account)

Conditional

汇丰银行（中国）电汇 · cost basis: Commission and cable are priced from HSBC's own tariff. HSBC's FX spread is not published in that document, so the all-in figure for this rail is deliberately left blank.

The same legal route as a BOC wire, run through HSBC China. Its published commission band is wider (min CNY 100, max CNY 500) and the cable fee is CNY 120. Premier status changes some fees and unlocks HSBC Global Transfers.

Pros
  • Published, capped tariff; English-language service; Premier relationship managers handle documentation.
  • Global Transfers between your own HSBC accounts can be free for Premier customers, which removes the sender commission.
  • Incoming foreign-currency wires into an HSBC China Premier account are listed as free on the same tariff.
Cons
  • Premier status normally needs CNY 500,000 of average daily balances or an equivalent qualifying relationship; otherwise you pay Advance-tier fees.
  • The Premier fee waiver applies to HSBC's own service, not to correspondent-bank charges.
  • HSBC China stopped issuing foreign-currency drafts in December 2019, so the draft workaround does not exist there.
  • Fewer branches than BOC; eligibility for the underlying remittance is still decided under the same SAFE rules.
  • China tax: Same as any other rail: no conversion tax; tax on income or gain only.
  • US tax: Same as any other rail: no tax on principal; USD 200 FX-gain threshold; worldwide income reporting.
  • US reporting: FBAR; Form 8938; Form 1116

HSBC Global Transfer between your own HSBC accounts

Conditional

汇丰环球转账同名速汇 · cost basis: Service fee is zero for Premier by tariff; the FX spread is not verified for HSBC, so the all-in total is blank rather than guessed.

A same-name transfer from HSBC China to your HSBC account in another country. The service fee is waived for Premier, but the money still has to be lawfully remittable and still gets converted at HSBC's rate.

Pros
  • Zero HSBC service fee for Premier customers per the published tariff.
  • Same-name routing removes a class of beneficiary-name errors.
  • Faster and simpler than a correspondent-bank telegraphic transfer.
Cons
  • Only works if you already hold an HSBC account in the destination country, which is itself an onboarding hurdle for US residents.
  • A waived service fee is not a waived FX spread; the conversion still costs roughly the bank's bid-ask spread.
  • The Advance tier is quoted a reference price of CNY 145 (about USD 20) per transfer.
  • It does not create an entitlement: if the funds are not remittable under SAFE rules, the free rail is irrelevant.
  • China tax: No conversion tax.
  • US tax: No tax on principal; USD 200 FX-gain threshold applies.
  • US reporting: FBAR; Form 8938

Wise CNY-to-USD transfer

Conditional

Wise 人民币汇出 · cost basis: Not priced. Wise states the fee differs by delivery method and is only shown when a transfer is set up, so any number here would be invented.

A licensed payment provider route out of China. Eligibility is narrow and specific: not a mainland Chinese national, must have worked in China, must hold a China tax record showing tax paid within the last three years, may only send to an account you own, and is capped at CNY 300,000 per transfer and at net salary overall.

Pros
  • Mid-market-style pricing with the fee shown before you confirm, rather than a bank's posted board rate.
  • Entirely app-based; no branch visit once verified.
  • Transparent per-transfer limit of CNY 300,000 makes planning arithmetic straightforward.
Cons
  • A mainland Chinese national cannot use the outbound rail at all, per Wise's own guide.
  • Requires tax records showing tax paid in the last three years; the limit is derived from net salary.
  • You may only send to another account you own, not to a family member or a third party.
  • Wise publishes no fixed fee for this corridor; the price is only knowable at quote time, so no cost column is computed here.
  • China tax: No conversion tax. The corridor is built around proof that China tax has already been paid on the salary being remitted.
  • US tax: No tax on principal; the salary was already taxable when earned.
  • US reporting: FBAR; Form 8938

Overseas-person route: remit lawful RMB income earned in China

Lawful

境外个人合法人民币收入购汇 · cost basis: Same bank tariff as the wire rails; not separately computed.

If you are an overseas individual with lawful current-account RMB income in China, SAFE Huifa [2007] No.1 Art. 13 lets you buy FX against your ID plus transaction evidence including tax proof. This is the clause that most directly fits a foreign national who earned money in China.

Pros
  • Explicitly written for non-resident/overseas individuals rather than domestic ones.
  • Not limited to the USD 50,000 annual total, because it is the documented above-total current-account path.
  • Tax proof is the key document, which you should already have.
Cons
  • Requires the money to be current-account income that was actually earned and taxed; it does not cover accumulated capital.
  • Individual banks interpret the evidentiary standard differently.
  • The unused-RMB conversion-back limb has a 24-month validity window tied to the original exchange receipt.
  • China tax: Income tax already withheld or paid; the remittance does not create a second charge.
  • US tax: The salary was taxable to the US when earned; a foreign tax credit may offset the Chinese tax on the same income.
  • US reporting: FBAR; Form 8938; Form 1116

Emigrant property transfer (移民财产转移)

Lawful

移民财产对外转移 · cost basis: Government approval is not a fee; the bank tariff applies at the remittance step. No separate government charge was published in the sources read.

The route built for exactly this problem: a person who emigrated from mainland China applies to SAFE to remit the lawful assets they owned before acquiring emigrant status. Since 2014 it no longer has to be released in stages, and the CNY 500,000 central filing threshold is gone.

Pros
  • Purpose-built for moving an entire pre-emigration estate, including property proceeds.
  • No staged-release requirement after the 2014 reform: one approval, then once or in instalments.
  • Notarisation of property-rights documents is no longer required.
  • Priced like an ordinary FX purchase and wire once approved.
Cons
  • Only available if you actually hold emigrant status and can show the assets predate it, and household registration cancellation is part of the evidence.
  • Requires a China tax clearance or tax-payment certificate before SAFE will approve.
  • Approval is discretionary and runs through the SAFE branch where your household registration was.
  • The published 2004 text still contains the superseded staged rules, so a bank may quote the old procedure until corrected.
  • China tax: Asset-transfer tax is settled before the tax clearance is issued: 20% flat on transfer income under IIT Law Art. 3(3), with the actual taxable base depending on cost basis and permitted deductions.
  • US tax: Remitting your own pre-emigration capital is not a US taxable event by itself; subsequent income and any FX gain over USD 200 are separate questions.
  • US reporting: FBAR; Form 8938; Form 3520 if the funds are a gift or bequest rather than your own

Inheritance transfer (继承财产转移)

Lawful

继承财产对外转移 · cost basis: Not priced: fees depend on the estate, the notary and the local SAFE branch, and no first-party fee schedule was located.

A foreign citizen who has lawfully inherited a Chinese estate applies at the decedent's former household-registration SAFE branch to liquidate and remit it. Since 2014, inheritances from different decedents can be combined into one application.

Pros
  • The only clean route for inherited Chinese assets held by a non-Chinese heir.
  • Post-2014 rules removed the separate-application requirement per decedent and the notarisation requirement for property documents.
  • Can be remitted in one go or in instalments once approved.
Cons
  • Requires a notarised or otherwise legally established inheritance entitlement.
  • Requires the decedent's tax clearance, which may mean dealing with an estate tax position you do not control.
  • SAFE will refuse anything subject to a pending civil or criminal case or with disputed ownership.
  • The published 2004 text and the official summary still contain pre-reform wording, which is a documented source inconsistency.
  • China tax: China has no inheritance tax in the sources read; the estate's asset-transfer tax and any unpaid income tax must be settled to obtain the tax clearance. That absence of an inheritance tax is stated narrowly: it is what these sources show, not a general assurance.
  • US tax: Inherited assets are generally not US income at receipt; later sale gain is measured from the inherited basis, and a bequest from a nonresident alien may need Form 3520 disclosure.
  • US reporting: Form 3520 Part IV if a reportable foreign bequest; FBAR; Form 8938

Sell Chinese property, then remit the proceeds

Conditional

房产出售后汇出 · cost basis: Not priced: the tax depends on basis and holding period, and the transaction taxes were not verified to a first-party rate table in this edition.

Convert the asset into CNY through a real sale, pay the transfer taxes, then use a bank or entitlement route to move the proceeds. This is how most large legitimate sums actually leave, and it is also where the 20% figure that people call a 'conversion penalty' really comes from.

Pros
  • Converts an illiquid asset into a remittable current-account/capital-account balance with a clean paper trail.
  • Produces exactly the contracts and tax receipts SAFE and the banks ask for.
  • Can be combined with the emigrant route if you qualify.
Cons
  • 20% flat IIT on the transfer gain is a real tax, not a conversion fee, and it is charged whether or not you ever move the money abroad.
  • Requires a genuine buyer, a registered contract and a tax clearance before the bank will remit.
  • The buyer's own FX position and local purchase restrictions affect timing.
  • Sale proceeds above the annual total still need a documented remittance basis.
  • China tax: 20% flat on income from transfer of assets under IIT Law Art. 3(3); additional transaction taxes may apply but were not verified in the sources read for this edition.
  • US tax: The sale is a disposition for US purposes; gain is computed using the USD basis, with a foreign tax credit potentially available for the Chinese tax on the same gain.
  • US reporting: Form 1116; Form 8949/Schedule D concepts apply but were not verified here; FBAR; Form 8938

  • US reporting: FBAR for the underlying accounts; FinCEN 105 if cash carried in exceeds USD 10,000

Carry physical cash out of China

Conditional

携带外币现钞出境 · cost basis: Not priced: the FX purchase leg uses the bank's cash rate, which differs from the remittance rate; the delivery cost is your own travel.

Legal only in small amounts with the right permit. Under USD 5,000 equivalent needs no carrying certificate; USD 5,000 to 10,000 needs a bank-issued certificate; above USD 10,000 is in principle not permitted and requires an exceptional SAFE permit. On the US side, over USD 10,000 must be declared on FinCEN 105.

Pros
  • No wire fee and no bank spread at the destination.
  • For a small amount, the paperwork is minimal.
Cons
  • Hard-capped; cannot solve a large transfer at all.
  • Physical loss and theft risk.
  • Two separate declaration regimes apply, one at each border.
  • Cash violations carry their own penalty of up to 20% of the amount under Reg. Art. 42.
  • China tax: No conversion tax; the cash is already converted.
  • US tax: No tax on carrying; declaration only.
  • US reporting: FinCEN 105 above USD 10,000; FBAR for accounts

Receive a gift from a Chinese relative

Conditional

亲属赠与 · cost basis: Same bank tariff as the wire rails.

A relative in China gifts you money and remits it. The US does not tax the receipt, but Form 3520 Part IV is required once aggregate gifts from a nonresident individual exceed USD 100,000 in a year. The Chinese side still has to satisfy the remittance rules.

Pros
  • No US income tax on a genuine gift.
  • Aggregation is per donor-and-related-parties, so the USD 100,000 threshold is not a per-transfer limit.
Cons
  • The giver still has to get the money out of China lawfully; the gift label does not bypass SAFE.
  • Missing the Form 3520 filing exposes you to penalties under IRC 6039F(c).
  • A gift dressed up as salary or a loan is a mischaracterisation risk on both sides.
  • China tax: The giving relative's position depends on how they obtained the money; China gift tax was not verified in this edition.
  • US tax: No income tax on a genuine gift; reporting is not taxation.
  • US reporting: Form 3520 Part IV above USD 100,000 aggregate; FBAR; Form 8938

Underground bank / 'private exchange' (地下钱庄)

Prohibited

地下钱庄 · cost basis: The statutory exposure is quantified on the Costs page as 30% and 100% of the amount, because that is what the regulation says.

No operational detail is published here. This is the route that produces the large 'penalty' people describe: the State Council FX Regulations make private or disguised FX trading, and evasion of FX controls, punishable by confiscation plus a fine of up to 30% of the amount, or 30% to 100% where serious, with criminal liability.

Pros
  • None. There is no lawful version of this.
Cons
  • Fine up to 30% of the amount, or 30%-100% where serious, plus confiscation (Reg. Arts. 39, 45).
  • Criminal liability is expressly preserved.
  • Counterparty failure risk is total: there is no regulator to complain to.
  • The US side treats an unreported foreign account and any unreported income as separate violations.
  • China tax: Not applicable: this is a penalty exposure, not a tax.
  • US tax: Any income moved this way remains taxable, and the account remains FBAR-reportable.
  • US reporting: FBAR; Form 8938

Splitting a large sum across relatives' or friends' USD 50,000 quotas

Prohibited

分拆结售汇 · cost basis: Not priced; penalty exposure modelled separately.

No operational detail is published here. The annual total is a per-person limit; moving one person's money through several people's identities to stay under it is the conduct the authenticity rule and the evasion provisions are aimed at.

Pros
  • None that survives examination.
Cons
  • Banks must review authenticity and may not process forged or altered transactions (Huifa [2007] No.1 Art. 6).
  • Evasion and disguised trading carry fines of up to 30%, or 30%-100% where serious (Reg. Arts. 39, 45).
  • Relatives who lend their identity take on the exposure too.
  • It converts a documentation problem into a criminal exposure problem.
  • China tax: Not applicable.
  • US tax: Gift and income characterisation issues still apply.
  • US reporting: FBAR; Form 3520 if gifts
