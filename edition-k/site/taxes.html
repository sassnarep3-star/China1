Tax, reporting and the real penalty exposure

Converting currency is not a taxable event in either country. What follows is what is taxed, what is merely reportable, and what is penalised — kept strictly apart, because conflating them is the single most expensive misunderstanding in this problem.

The three things people confuse

1 · Cost of converting
0.210% + capped fees
A bank spread and a few fixed fees. Small, published, unavoidable if you use a bank.
2 · Tax on income or gain
20% / 3%–45%
Owed because of what the money is, not because you moved it. Owed even if you leave it in China.
3 · Penalty for breaking the rules
20%–100%
Only if you use a prohibited route or fail to report. Entirely avoidable by not doing that.

Why there is no "conversion tax" line. The China IIT Law taxes nine categories of income, and exchanging one currency for another is not one of them (S10). US law taxes foreign-currency gain only when the currency is disposed of, and for a personal transaction it does not recognise that gain at all unless it exceeds $200 (S13). If someone has told you that converting CNY to USD costs 20% or 30%, they were describing either a real asset-transfer gain or an illegal-channel fine.

2. Statutory penalty exposure by amount

The FX Regulations set fines as a percentage of the amount moved: up to 30% of the amount for evasion or disguised FX trading, and 30% up to the full equivalent where the circumstances are serious, with confiscation of unlawful gains and criminal liability expressly preserved (S08). Cash violations are capped at 20%.

Arithmetic on the statutory percentages, not a prediction. Whether an offence exists, and whether circumstances are 'serious', is decided by the authority.

4. The foreign tax credit, in one paragraph

If China taxes income that the US also taxes, you generally claim a credit on Form 1116 rather than deducting it, so the same income is not taxed twice (S15). The credit is limited and it does not refund Chinese tax. It is not available for tax on income you excluded from US gross income. Critically, the credit does not convert a Chinese tax into a US one, and it never applies to a fine.
