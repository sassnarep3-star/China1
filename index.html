Executive summary

You are not being taxed to convert. You are being asked to prove what the money is.

No Chinese rule taxes the act of turning RMB into USD — I searched the FX Regulations, the PBoC individual-FX measures, the 2020 Current Account Guidelines and the Individual Income Tax Law and found none. What actually blocks you is different, and better: as a US citizen you are classified as a 境外个人 (overseas individual), which means you get no $50,000 purchase quota at all — and, in exchange, no cap either, once the money is documented.

The expensive part is not the conversion. It is the US penalty for not reporting the account — up to $16,536 per year if non-willful, $165,353 if willful — which accrues whether or not you ever convert a single yuan. Fix that first.

The answer in four lines

1 · Identify the source of funds

This decides everything. Because you have no blanket quota, each yuan must be traceable to a category that permits conversion. Income, house sale, pre-emigration property and inheritance each have their own route — all uncapped, all document-gated.

See all 21 channels →

2 · Fix reporting before converting

FBAR and Form 8938 history first. If years were missed and the failure wasn't willful, the Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures are the intended route. Do not "quiet disclose".

3 · Get the tax clearance certificate

This is the hard gate. Without 完税凭证, both the emigrant transfer and the home-sale remittance fail outright, and documented-income remittances require tax documents too. Chinese IIT at 20% on a property gain is real money — budget for it.

4 · Never improvise a fifth route

Underground banks, splitting across relatives' quotas, false declarations and crypto bridges are all expressly prohibited — and the penalty attaches to the whole amount, up to 100%.
