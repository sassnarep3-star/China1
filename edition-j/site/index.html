Getting money out of China: what it actually costs — and what is actually taxed

For a US citizen holding CNY in a Chinese bank account, the FX conversion itself is not taxed in either country. The real costs are (1) the FX margin and wire fees you can compare, (2) any Chinese income tax that was never paid on the underlying money, and (3) US filing duties (FBAR, Form 8938, §988). The cheapest lawful route depends on one question: how was the money earned and what documents prove it?

Most important — read first: Do not try to optimize the FX rate before you know which route you are eligible for. A bank will first ask for transaction-amount proof and tax records (S01 Art.7, S02 Art.12–13, S08). If you can document the source, use the matching documented route — entitlement is the product. If you cannot, the $50k/year convenience quota (S02 Art.2) is the only simple lawful channel; splitting across relatives or using underground/crypto is prohibited (S01 Art.7, S08, S21) and carries criminal/penalty risk.
Bottom line on tax penalties: There is no single “40% conversion tax.” China IIT is on the original income categories (salary 3–45%, dividends/asset gains 20% per S09 Art.3) — not on converting. The US taxes worldwide income (IRS S15) but gives a foreign tax credit (S15) for qualifying Chinese taxes; mixing up the two systems creates the “considerable tax” fear. The FX move itself can create a small §988 gain only if CNY appreciated after you received it, and personal gains ≤$200 per transaction are excluded (S14 §988(e)(2)). FBAR/8938 are reporting, not extra tax.

How to read the 0.21%: BOC posts two prices (buy/sell). The midpoint is (669.03+666.22)/2 = 667.625 (S07). The one-way cost you pay over midpoint on a single conversion is (669.03-667.625)/667.625 = 0.2104%. The “0.42%” you may see elsewhere is the round-trip buy-then-sell spread — double-counting for one transfer. Fees are capped, so % falls as amount rises: 0.53% at $10k → 0.23% at $400k.
