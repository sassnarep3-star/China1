Penalty breakdown

The "conversion penalty", taken apart

People describe a large penalty for converting CNY to USD. There is no such tax. What exists is four different things that get added together in conversation: a bank spread you always pay, Chinese tax on the income, penalties for taking a prohibited route, and US penalties for not reporting the account. They are separated here so they can be compared honestly.

The asymmetry that matters

Put the largest lawful cost next to the largest unlawful one at the same balance. At USD 400,000 the entire published bank cost of moving the money is a few hundred dollars; the statutory maximum for one willful FBAR violation is 200,000. A prohibited route adds a Chinese fine of up to 30% (or 100% if serious) on top.

These are statutory maxima, not predictions: the actual fine is set case by case, and the safe number for any lawful route is zero. The comparison is the point - the penalty dwarfs the fee.

The trap in comparing to the central parity

A tempting shortcut is to measure the FX cost against the PBOC central parity of 6.7188 (2026-11-09 09:15). On the pair retrieved for this edition the result is -0.42% - a negative cost, which would mean the bank paid you to convert. That is not a market anomaly; it is what happens when you subtract a rate fixed at 09:15 on one day from a board published the next morning. The gap mixes the real spread with a day of market movement, so this edition does not use it. The defensible measures are the ones derived from a single board: the half-spread (0.21%) and the full spread (0.42%).

The one number people quote that is real. The 2004 property-transfer measures said first release no more than half, then half of the remainder after a year, then the rest after two. That looked like a "penalty for waiting". 汇发〔2014〕2号 cancelled it. If a bank tells you the staging applies, that is the outdated text, not the current rule.
