Jurisdiction: United States

The US side is where the real money is

A Chinese bank account holding RMB is a foreign financial account. It is reportable whether or not it earns a cent, and the penalties for not reporting it are an order of magnitude larger than any FX spread.

The asymmetry, in one comparison Converting a $100,000 balance at a 1% spread costs about $1,000. Leaving the same balance off three FBARs is three violations at up to $16,536 each if non-willful — roughly $49,608 — and far more if willful. Reporting compliance is worth about fifty times more than shopping for a better rate.

Forms that apply

IRC §988 — the "conversion tax" that mostly isn't one

What the statute says

  • §988(a)(1)(A): FX gain or loss on a §988 transaction is computed separately and treated as ordinary income or loss.
  • §988(e)(1): the preceding provisions "shall not apply to any section 988 transaction entered into by an individual which is a personal transaction."
  • §988(e)(2): no gain is recognised on a personal transaction in nonfunctional currency by reason of exchange-rate changes between acquisition and disposition — "shall not apply if the gain which would otherwise be recognized on the transaction exceeds $200."
  • §988(e)(3): a personal transaction excludes anything whose allocable expenses meet §162 (other than travel) or §212.

What it means in practice — with a caveat

Converting your own savings is not designed to create US income. Ordinary, documentable personal conversions fall outside §988 rather than triggering a tax.

But read the direction carefully: the exclusion is symmetric. If the RMB weakened against the dollar between acquisition and conversion, the resulting "loss" is likewise not deductible.

The caveat: the interaction of (e)(1) and (e)(2) is genuinely technical, and the answer can change if the money is connected to a business or investment activity rather than personal holdings. Get a CPA to confirm the characterisation for your facts. This page does not assert a conclusion beyond the quoted text.

The principle that resolves most of the anxiety Principal is not income. Moving your own after-tax money across a border is not a taxable event. What is taxable is (a) the income that produced it, and (b) any gain on the asset that was sold to produce it. Your Chinese IIT exposure lives in (a) and (b) — not in the wire.

The compliance route, in order

  1. Determine whether you were required to file. FBAR triggers at $10,000 aggregate at any point in the year. Form 8938 needs a filed income-tax return and the applicable threshold.
  2. Reconstruct the history. FBAR requires the maximum value during each year; 8938 requires the year-end value. Five years of records is the stated retention period for FBAR.
  3. If years were missed and the failure was not willful, use the Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures: a certification of non-willful conduct plus amended or delinquent returns. Available to both US residents and non-residents, but not if you are already under civil examination or criminal investigation.
  4. Do not "quiet disclose." Filing a bare amended return and hoping is a known trap: the IRS states that penalties previously assessed are not abated by later joining the streamlined procedures.
  5. Then — and only then — choose the conversion route on the Channels page.

Streamlined procedure details: S25. It is not a closing agreement — the IRS does not acknowledge receipt, and the returns remain auditable.
